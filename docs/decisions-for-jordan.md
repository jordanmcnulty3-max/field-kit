# Field Kit v6: decisions for Jordan

The medical content raised 147 open questions while it was written and reviewed. All of them, with the sources
on each side, are in `docs/content-review.md` under "Open questions for Jordan". Most can stand as written:
the app already follows the higher-authority source and says so.

This page pulls out the ones worth your attention. Numbers in brackets are the question's position in that list.

Nothing here blocks the release. Each item says what the app does today; answer only the ones you want changed.

## A. Yours to decide (group preference)

1. **Open chest wound: seal it or leave it open?** [2]
   Today: follows AHA/Red Cross. Leave it open or use a dressing that does not seal; a vented chest seal only
   for trained people who carry one. The military guidelines (TCCC) say always seal.
   Change it if your group carries vented chest seals and trains on them.

2. **Alone with an adult in cardiac arrest, no signal.** [9]
   Today: the step states AHA's advice (go for help first, then CPR) and the off-grid trade-off (help may be
   hours away), and leaves the choice to the rescuer. Tell me if you want the app to pick one.

3. **Does every faint mean evacuation?** [110, 111]
   Today: yes, anyone who actually passed out is sent to medical care, as AHA and Red Cross say.
   Off-grid that will end trips for simple faints. A narrower group rule is yours to set.

4. **Vomiting and diarrhea: when to evacuate.** [108]
   Today: the Red Cross signs of dehydration lead (cannot keep fluids down, dizzy, little or no urine, blood,
   bad belly pain). The stricter published counts ("3 or more vomits in a day") are shown as information only.

5. **Heat stroke: evacuate every case?** [43]
   Today: yes (AHA). Wilderness guidance allows release after 2 hours if fully recovered.

6. **Tourniquets on long evacuations.** [117, 118, 135, 140, 141]
   Today: converting a tourniquet to a pressure dressing is an advanced procedure for trained members only,
   within 2 hours of the first tourniquet (later only under a medic's direction), never after 6 hours, and the
   app does not allow a second try after a re-bleed even though TCCC permits one. Repositioning is also refused
   after 6 hours, which is the app's caution, not TCCC's.
   Decide who in the group, if anyone, is allowed to convert.

7. **Walking out when nobody can carry the casualty.** [57, 66, 91]
   Chest pain, snakebite and frostbitten feet all reach this. Today each step states what the source advises
   (rest, keep still, do not walk on thawed feet) and the off-grid trade-off. For frostbite the app says not to
   rewarm the feet before a walk-out.

8. **Many stings.** [69]
   Today: dozens of bee, wasp, yellow jacket or hornet stings, or a child with several, means evacuate; so does
   feeling ill after multiple stings. The only published numbers are the amounts that kill, so this threshold is
   a judgement. Fire ants are not counted.

9. **Rabies from squirrels, rabbits and pets you can watch.** [88]
   Today: any wild animal bite is treated as a possible rabies exposure. The CDC allows a healthy dog, cat or
   ferret to be watched for 10 days instead.

10. **Over-the-counter pain relief.** [32, 47, 68, 84]
    Today: mentioned without a dose where the AHA first aid guideline recommends it (small burns, eye
    discomfort, stings), with exclusions for shock, head injury and reduced alertness. Left out for altitude
    headache. This is slightly wider than the four medicines in the original design. Say if you want it removed
    or extended.

11. **Sleeping pills at altitude.** [62]
    Today: the app says take no sedating medicine to sleep unless a prescriber has said it is safe at altitude,
    labelled as the group's caution. The CDC considers some short-acting prescription ones generally safe.

12. **Pelvic injury, untrained person.** [143]
    Today: no tight binder, but a wide sheet or blanket may be tied snugly around the hips (Red Cross
    wilderness guide). Trained members get the full binder procedure.

13. **Traction splint is not in the app.** [127]
    It was on the list you approved. TCCC dropped the skill in 2024 and no top-tier source gives the steps.
    Searching "traction splint" lands on Broken bone. Say if you want a pointer entry kept.

14. **9-line defaults to peacetime.** [134]
    Line 6 is the number and type of wounds; line 9 is terrain. Wartime mode is one tap away.

## B. Worth asking a medical professional

No published source answers these, so the app's wording is a labelled judgement. If anyone in your circle is a
medic, nurse, paramedic or doctor, these are the ones to show them.

1. **Surgical airway that fails.** [122, 136] The app says: do not ventilate through a tube with no misting,
   chest rise or breath sounds; keep the opening controlled; use a bag-valve-mask over the mouth and nose if the
   face allows; evacuate now. It does not say whether to leave the tube in or take it out, or how to control an
   opening with nothing in it. It also requires a bag-valve-mask before starting and allows two tube attempts.
2. **Possible spine injury and vomiting, especially for a lone rescuer.** [7, 16, 34] The app rolls the casualty
   as one unit with a helper. No source covers a rescuer on their own.
3. **Discoloured epinephrine auto-injector when it is the only one.** [6] The app states only that the Red Cross
   says to discard it.
4. **Very cold casualty with no signs of life.** [37, 54] Wilderness guidance checks breathing for a full minute
   before CPR; AHA's lay rule is not to spend time on a pulse check. Also when to stop CPR after cold-water
   drowning.
5. **Heat stroke after the 15-minute immersion limit.** [59, 60, 61] The app takes them out and keeps cooling by
   soaking and fanning; stops when they think clearly or start shivering; and says no more plain water for a
   heavy drinker who is getting worse.
6. **Limb with no pulse after rewrapping a pressure bandage.** [138] The app says keep it firm enough to control
   bleeding and evacuate urgently.
7. **Dislocation with poor circulation beyond it.** [131, 147] The app allows one reduction attempt, then splints
   as it lies and evacuates rapidly.
8. **Heavily bleeding belly wound.** [13, 30] The app uses firm pressure and does not guide packing; it never
   cleans a wound that needed a tourniquet, packing or a pressure bandage.
9. **Swallowed a caustic product, no signal.** [78, 79] Poison Control's own advice is a small drink of water or
   milk for someone awake who can swallow; the app gives that only as Poison Control's instruction.
10. **Someone revived with naloxone who wants to leave.** [113] The app says stay with them, do not restrain
    them, and that naloxone wears off before the opioid does.

## C. Calls I made that you should know about

- **Stroke signs in a known diabetic with no glucose meter:** if fully awake and able to swallow, give sugar and
  keep evacuating as a stroke. [94]
- **Chemical in the eye:** the poisoning protocol starts the rinse and hands over to the Eye injury protocol, so
  there is one rule. [87]
- **Fumes:** a quick reach into a tent or vehicle on a held breath is allowed; going into a pit, tank, well, rock
  cave or mine full of gas is not. [86]
- **Frostbite rewarming water:** 99 to 102°F, the range both sources accept. [39]
- **Impaled objects:** left in place, except a small fishhook near the surface; an object blocking the airway is
  mentioned with its lower-tier source. [24, 35]
- **Diagrams:** eight are in. The recovery-position drawing was removed because it did not read clearly.

## D. Survival content (v7 Tasks 13 to 16; the water and sanitation pack from D15)

Source disagreements found while checking the Survival topics. Each says what the app does today.

1. **How long to boil water.** EPA and CDC: a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes higher up). NYS DEC's state
   land camping rules and the Army survival manuals (ATP 3-50.21, FM 21-76-1) say 5 minutes. Today: 1 minute,
   following EPA and CDC. Say if the group should boil 5 minutes. (To change it: surv-make-water-safe, surv-wells-without-power
   and the protocol p:vomiting-diarrhea.)
2. **Altitude for the 3-minute boil.** EPA: above 5,000 ft. CDC: above 6,500 ft. Army ATP 3-50.21: above 8,000 ft.
   Today: 5,000 ft (most cautious; several Adirondack High Peaks are above it). (To change it: surv-make-water-safe,
   surv-wells-without-power and p:vomiting-diarrhea.)
3. **Bleach dose for 8.25% bleach.** EPA: 6 drops per gallon. CDC: 8 drops per gallon for any 5% to 9% bleach.
   Today: 8 drops per gallon for both 6% and 8.25% (the larger dose).
4. **Rainwater.** Army ATP 3-50.21: fresh rain caught straight into clean containers needs no treatment. CDC:
   rainwater must be treated. Today: treat it (CDC).
5. **Chlorine dioxide tablets and Cryptosporidium.** CDC's emergency water page: chlorine dioxide tablets kill
   Cryptosporidium if you follow the instructions. CDC's hiking and camping page: they kill Giardia but are only
   somewhat effective against Cryptosporidium, and filtering first is safest. Today: the app says they work better
   than iodine or chlorine but not fully, follow the label exactly, and boil or filter first if you can.
6. **When mountain thunderstorms start.** NWS: they typically develop in the early afternoon; be down the mountain
   by noon. CDC: most common in the late afternoon or evening. Today: be down by noon (NWS, the earlier time).
7. **Ferro rod technique.** The old Fire topic said to hold the striker still and pull the rod back. No
   authoritative source opened says that; Army ATP 3-50.21 says to drive the striker down the rod with even
   pressure. Today: the Army method. Say if the group trains the pull-back method and wants it back (it would need a
   source).
8. **Fire next to a shelter.** Smokey Bear: keep campfires at least 15 feet from tents, vegetation and low branches.
   The Army manuals put a survival fire in front of a lean-to with a reflector wall (and about 3 feet from a snow
   cave opening). Today: the Shelter topic keeps the Army lean-to fire; the 15-foot rule is not added.
9. **Lightning protocol: the crouch, and when mountain storms happen (question for a medical professional).**
   src/protocols/lightning.json keeps the lightning crouch (feet together, or sitting on a pad with feet off the
   ground) as a last resort, citing the Wilderness Medical Society. The NWS page "The National Weather Service
   (NWS) stopped recommending the crouch in 2008" (https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-crouch) says it
   "simply doesn't provide a significant level of protection." The same protocol credits CDC with storms being
   most common on "summer afternoons"; CDC's Safety Guidelines: Lightning says late afternoon or evening (NWS says
   early afternoon). Today: the protocol is unchanged. Should the crouch stay as a last resort, and should the
   timing line be corrected to the sources?
10. **Cooking temperature for wild game.** CDC (MMWR 2024, bear meat trichinellosis): wild game meat to 165°F or
   more. NYS Department of Health: all game and birds to 165°F. USDA FSIS ("Roasting Those 'Other' Holiday Meats"):
   venison roasts 160°F, whole game birds 165°F (and all poultry 165°F on its chart). Missouri Department of Conservation: 160°F. Penn State Extension: whole venison
   cuts 145°F, ground 160°F. Pennsylvania Game Commission (tularemia page): at least 135°F. Army ATP 3-50.21: 180°F.
   Today: 165°F for all wild game (CDC, NYS DOH) and birds (NYS DOH, USDA FSIS), fish 145°F (USDA FSIS), and freezing is not treated as
   killing trichinella in wild game (CDC; Penn State says three weeks of freezing works, which CDC says is not
   reliable for wild game). Say if the group wants the Army's 180°F.
11. **Wild plants removed for lack of a source.** Dandelion: University of Vermont and University of Maine Extension
   confirm it is edible, but no opened source names its look-alikes or says it has no dangerous ones, so under the
   plant rule (each edible named with its dangerous look-alikes) it was removed. Acorns: only the Army manuals give
   the leaching method; no extension or state source was found, so they were removed. Today: the topic names
   cattail (with two poisonous look-alikes, yellow flag iris and the native blue flag iris) and wild blackberries
   and raspberries only. Say if you want either back; it needs a source (or plant photos at pack 4).
12. **Bear spray in New York.** The Northeast Black Bear Technical Committee says bear spray can work with practice
   and that local rules may restrict carrying it. Whether New York law allows carrying bear spray was not checked
   (no law page was opened). Today: the topic says local rules may restrict it. Say if the group carries bear
   spray, so pack 2 can check the law.
13. **Tourniquet in the ten essentials.** The old Ten essentials topic said "First aid kit, including a tourniquet".
   None of the lists opened (The Mountaineers, National Park Service, NYS DEC) names a tourniquet, and no
   authoritative hiking source was found that does. Today: the topic and checklist say "First aid kit". The
   Emergency section still has the tourniquet protocols. Say if the group wants a tourniquet listed as its own kit
   rule (it would be the group's choice, recorded as such, not a sourced Ten essentials item).
14. **Knots: joining ropes and holding people.** Army ATP 3-50.21 and Scout Life give the square knot as a way to
   join two ropes of equal size; Animated Knots (citing Ashley) says never use it to join ropes for anything that
   matters, because it can capsize. Scout Life also calls the bowline the knot for tying a rope around someone
   needing rescue; Animated Knots says a bowline shakes loose and needs a safety knot for climbing. Today: the more
   cautious sources are used. The square knot is for bandages and bundles only, and the Knots topic says it does
   not teach knots for climbing, rappelling or holding a person's weight. Say if the group wants a life-safety
   knot section; it would need its own sourced pack (and verified diagrams).
15. **How many days of water to store (water planner) — Water pack Task 3.** CDC: 3 days, and a 2-week
   supply if possible. Red Cross: 2 weeks at home, 3 days for evacuation. FEMA, Red Cross and USDA (Food and Water
   in an Emergency): at least 2 weeks. NYS DHSES: be prepared to make it on your own for at least 10 days
   (its kit list says supplies to last up to 10 days). NYS DOH leaflet (2014): 3 days.
   Ready.gov: several days. Today: the planner's default is 14 days (the longest target; all agree on 1 gallon per
   person per day). Say if the group wants a longer default for a long grid-down period; no source opened gives one.
16. **Extra water in very hot weather (water planner) — Water pack Task 3.** Ready.gov Water: a normally active
   person needs about three quarters of a gallon of fluid a day, and "in very hot temperatures, water needs can
   double". FEMA, Red Cross and USDA (Food and Water in an Emergency): at least two quarts of drinking water a day,
   and hot environments can double that amount, so FEMA's extra is two quarts (half a gallon). Ready.gov is the
   ambiguous one: it does not say which amount doubles, so its extra could be three quarters of a gallon (its fluid
   figure) or a whole gallon (the stored gallon). Today: the planner adds 1 gallon per person per day when "Very hot
   weather" is ticked (the largest reading). Say if the group wants the smaller figure. No source opened gives an amount for children, nursing or
   pregnant women or sick people, and CDC, FEMA and Ready.gov give none for pets; Ohio State University Extension
   (HYG-5354) says to store 1 quart per small pet, which the planner quotes in its note but does not add, because
   it applies extras per person.
17. **Toilet tank water (surv-water-in-the-house) — Water pack Task 4.** CDC and FEMA (2017 fact sheet): the toilet
   tank (not the bowl) is a usable source if the water is clear and has no toilet cleaner in it; FEMA, the Red
   Cross and USDA call it a last resort. An unattributed copy of the old Ready.gov "Managing Water" text lists
   "water from the toilet bowl or flush tank" as unsafe. Today: the topic allows the tank as a last resort and says
   to treat it before drinking (more cautious than CDC). Say if the group wants it ruled out, or allowed untreated.
   Fix round 1: the Regional Water Providers Consortium (Emergency Water Sources) also lists "water from your toilet
   bowl or tank" under "Do Not Drink", starred as a possible sanitation source.
18. **Bleach for stored well water (surv-water-store) — Water pack Task 4.** Ready.gov (Storing Water in Plastic Soda
   Bottles): add two drops of bleach to well or unchlorinated water, without saying how much water (a soda bottle of
   unstated size). Washington State DOH: one or two drops per gallon to keep stored water fresh. The v7 dose (EPA,
   CDC) is 8 drops of 5% to 9% bleach per gallon. Today: the topic sends well water to Make water safe for the full
   v7 dose before storing. Say if the group wants a lower "storage" dose for water already known to be safe.
   Fix round 1: the National Disaster Education Coalition (Red Cross, FEMA and others) gives a different dose again,
   16 drops per gallon of 5.25% bleach, for water of questionable purity. The Red Cross (Disaster Supplies Kit, via
   University of Missouri Extension) says some sources have contaminants bleach cannot neutralize and only the
   local public health agency should advise on storing and treating them; the topic now says "make it safe" (not
   "with bleach") and tells private-well owners to ask their health department in normal times.
19. **Waterbed water (surv-water-in-the-house) — Water pack Task 4.** FEMA (2017 fact sheet): waterbeds are a source
   not to use (fungicides in the water, chemicals in the vinyl). Louisiana's emergency office: not for drinking, but
   safe for hand washing and laundry. Today: "Don't use it" (FEMA). Say if the group wants to allow it for laundry.
   Fix round 1: the Regional Water Providers Consortium (Emergency Water Sources) also lists radiators and water beds
   under "Do Not Drink"; it stars water beds (and toilet water) as possible sanitation sources.
20. **Containers and bottled water (surv-water-store) — Water pack Task 4.** Glass: CDC says not glass; FEMA, Red Cross
   and USDA, Ohio State and Washington State DOH allow glass. Today: no glass (CDC; also Red Cross: glass breaks).
   Bottled water: CDC says observe the expiration date; the Red Cross says replace it at least once a year. Today:
   whichever comes first. Sanitizing: CDC pours the bleach solution out and lets the container air-dry; Ready.gov
   rinses it out with clean water. Today: CDC's steps (no rinse). Say if any of these should change.
21. **Gas water heater before draining: off or pilot (surv-water-in-the-house) — Water pack Task 4 fix round 1.**
   FEMA, the Red Cross and USDA (Food and Water in an Emergency): be sure the electricity or gas is off. The Regional
   Water Providers Consortium water heater hanger: turn the gas knob "to the pilot setting — do not turn it
   completely off". Today: "off" (FEMA), and relight by the heater's or gas company's instructions. Say if the group
   wants "pilot" instead, which avoids relighting but leaves a flame under a tank being drained.
22. **Rainwater for drinking (surv-find-water, surv-make-water-safe) — Water pack Task 5, revised in fix round 1.**
   FEMA (Where can I find clean drinking water after a disaster?): do not drink collected rainwater. Lake Champlain
   Sea Grant (2026): harvested rainwater is unsafe for drinking, cooking or washing. CDC (Collecting Rainwater): not
   necessarily safe to drink without first removing germs and chemicals. CDC (Find Clean Water) and FEMA with the Red
   Cross and USDA (Food and Water in an Emergency) list rainwater among sources to treat. Virginia Cooperative
   Extension: water from wood, asphalt and tar shingle roofs may only suit irrigation. Today: rain caught directly on
   a clean tarp or in a clean container is treated like any wild water (v7, D4); rain off a roof is for flushing
   toilets and cleaning, is treated before washing hands, dishes or food, and is drunk only if nothing safer is
   available, filtered and then boiled or disinfected. (The FEMA Q&A does not say "roof"; the app applies it to roof
   run-off.) Say if the group wants roof rainwater ruled out for drinking altogether, or a roof-washer and treatment
   set-up described (pack 6).
23. **Filter size for bacteria (surv-make-water-safe) — Water pack Task 5. Withdrawn in fix round 1.** Colorado State
   University Extension's own table gives 0.3 micron or smaller for bacteria, as CDC does (only its text says under
   0.1 micron). No real disagreement; the topic gives CDC's 0.3 micron. Number kept so later items do not shift.
24. **Sunlight (SODIS) time (surv-make-water-safe) — Water pack Task 5, revised in fix round 1.** CDC: 6 hours in sun,
   or 2 days if cloudy (no latitude given). WHO Technical Note 5: 24 hours on a sunny day or 48 hours if cloudy, "in
   areas near the equator". No opened source gives a time for New York's latitude. Today: at least WHO's times, sunlight
   is a last resort, and the topic says no source gives a time for New York, so treat it as unreliable there
   (fix round 2). Say if the
   group wants sunlight dropped altogether.
25. **Disinfecting a well (surv-wells-without-power) — Water pack Task 5.** (a) CDC's current table prints 3 1/4 cups
   of bleach for 30 ft of water in a 10 in casing; CDC's earlier version and its own metric table (888 mL) give
   3 3/4 cups, which fits the rest of the column. Today: 3 3/4 cups, with CDC's printed value noted. (b) Standing time:
   CDC at least 12 hours; NYS DOH at least 8, preferably 12 to 24; EPA 6 to 24. Today: at least 12 hours, NYS DOH's
   12 to 24 noted. (c) NYS DOH's own leaflet uses a fixed amount (2 quarts of bleach in 10 gallons of water,
   recirculated with the pump for an hour, then 2 more quarts) instead of a table; it needs a working pump. Today:
   CDC's table and steps. (d) With no pump, CDC gives only "bail with a bucket" and "pour water back with a bucket";
   the topic adds: let it stand 12 hours, then bail until the chlorine smell is gone ("CDC gives no other no-pump
   steps. Do this:"), with goggles
   or a face shield (CDC) and never climbing into the well (WHO). (e) Fix round 1: CDC's step mixes the bleach with 3
   to 5 gallons of water in a 5-gallon bucket, but its tables reach 10 gallons (drilled) and 13 3/4 gallons (dug) of
   bleach alone; CDC does not say what to do then, and no opened source does. Fix round 2: the topic now says to
   mix and pour in several batches until all the bleach from the table is in, and not to use less bleach to make it
   fit, marked "CDC does not cover this". Say if a well
   contractor or the health department should check (d) and (e) before the group relies on them.
26. **Springs (surv-find-water, surv-wells-without-power) — Water pack Task 5.** Army ATP 3-50.21: springs are
   "typically the safest open source". Penn State Extension: springs are highly susceptible to contamination and most
   need continuous disinfection. Today: treat spring water like any other (Penn State).
27. **Bleach in toilet buckets (surv-toilet-no-water) — Water pack Task 6.** FEMA (Are You Ready?): after each use
   pour a small amount of household disinfectant such as chlorine bleach into the toilet container. Anchorage and
   Wayne County (Michigan) emergency management: bleach diluted 1 to 10, with no bleach strength. The two-bucket
   agencies (Oregon State Extension, Jefferson and Clallam counties, Washington County, Wilsonville) use only cover
   material. Washington State Department of Health: urine contains ammonia, and bleach plus ammonia makes chloramine
   gas. Today: no bleach in any waste bucket; bleach only to clean an emptied bucket (CDC's 1 cup in 5 gallons for
   household items). Say if the group wants a disinfectant in the poo bucket.
28. **Latrine distances (surv-latrines, surv-toilet-no-water) — Water pack Task 6.** From water: King County, Oregon
   State Extension and the Army 100 ft; Sphere 30 m; NYS DEC (state land rule) 150 ft; Leave No Trace, US Forest
   Service and NPS 200 ft for cat holes. Today: 200 ft for latrines and buried waste too. Pit bottom above
   groundwater: King County 1 ft, Sphere 1.5 m; today 1.5 m. Close the pit: Army at 1 ft from the top, Sphere leaves
   0.5 m; today 0.5 m. From the kitchen: King County 100 ft from homes and campsites; the Army 100 yards downwind of
   its field kitchen (Army alone, so not used). Sphere also sets a maximum of 50 m from dwellings to a shared toilet,
   so people can reach it at night; not stated. Say if the group wants the Army's 100 yards from the kitchen or
   Sphere's 50 m maximum added.
29. **Latrines and buried waste in town (surv-latrines, surv-toilet-no-water) — Water pack Task 6.** King County:
   latrines are not appropriate in urban places, but bagged waste may be buried on your own property (no depth or
   distance given). Jefferson County: do not dig a pit privy; with seasonally saturated soils, germs spread to
   neighbouring properties. Oregon State Extension: in more rural areas consider a latrine or hole and bury the poo.
   Today: surv-latrines is tagged rural, woods and coast (hidden under the town filter); in town, don't bury or dig,
   hold the bagged waste in its can and wait (surv-toilet-no-water says so). Outside town, bury it at least 3 ft deep
   (King County's latrine depth; no source gives a depth for bagged waste) and at least 200 ft from water. Say if a
   backyard latrine in town should be allowed when the sewer will be dead for months, or if a different burial depth
   is wanted.
30. **Flushing after a disaster (surv-toilet-no-water) — Water pack Task 6.** Oregon State Extension: avoid toilets even
   if your home sewer lines are not damaged; the street pipes may be broken. DC Water and NPS describe bucket
   flushing during a water outage. Jefferson County: don't flush after a disaster until officials say it is OK.
   Today: flush with a bucket only while the sewer works and nothing backs up; after an earthquake, flood or similar
   damage, don't flush until officials say so. Town sewers often run on electric lift stations: West Hants Regional
   Municipality (Nova Scotia) and Charlotte County Utilities (Florida) tell customers to cut water use at once when
   the power goes out, because the sewer fills and backs up into basements. Fix round 1 added this, with "in a long
   blackout, expect the sewer to stop and switch to the bucket toilet". Say if the group wants "never flush in a
   long outage" instead.
31. **Burying household garbage (surv-garbage-and-greywater) — Water pack Task 6.** FEMA (Are You Ready?): "Bury
   garbage, if necessary", no depth or distance. The Army: a 4 x 4 x 4 ft pit at least 100 yards downstream of any
   water in use (Army alone). Leave No Trace: buried garbage still draws animals. Today: no burying instructions;
   bag, lid and store. Say if the group wants the Army's pit described.
32. **How long a sick person stays away from food, water and the group (surv-stomach-illness-in-a-group,
   p:vomiting-diarrhea) — Water pack Task 7.** CDC (norovirus): don't prepare or handle food or care for others; "Wait
   at least 2 days (48 hours) after symptoms stop." CDC (evacuation centers, archived 2019): separate the sick, and keep
   ill workers off, "until 24 hours after diarrhea and vomiting stop". Minnesota Department of Health (shelters,
   2025) and University of Minnesota Extension: 72 hours, for food handling and for isolation. The Emergency protocol
   Severe vomiting or diarrhea says 2 days (CDC) and was not changed (medical content). Today: the topic agrees with
   the protocol: at least 2 days after symptoms stop (CDC), and 3 days if the group can manage it (Minnesota); the
   24-hour figure is not in the text. The reviewer recommends 3 days (72 hours) everywhere, protocol included, as the
   more cautious figure that meets both CDC and Minnesota. Say whether to move the whole app, protocol and topic, to
   3 days. (Places to change: surv-stomach-illness-in-a-group summary, doFirst, the warning in "Food and water" and the
   whole-group rule in "How long to keep it up" ("Keep the sick person apart" points to the food rule and gives Minnesota's
   72 hours as its isolation period); and p:vomiting-diarrhea steps 8 and 10 and its don'ts.)
33. **Bleach for vomit and diarrhea (surv-clean-and-disinfect) — Water pack Task 7.** CDC (norovirus): 1,000 to 5,000
   ppm, "5 to 25 tablespoons of household bleach [5% to 8%] per gallon", at least 5 minutes. CDC (evacuation centers,
   archived 2019): 1,000 ppm, 5 tablespoons of 6% per gallon, keep wet 10 minutes if possible. Minnesota Department of
   Health: 1/3 cup per gallon (1,000 ppm, no strength printed). University of Minnesota Extension: about 1,000 ppm is
   1/3 cup of 5.25%, 1/4 cup of 6%, or 2 1/2 tablespoons of 8.25% per gallon. 1/3 cup (5 tablespoons) of 5% to 9% per
   gallon is also CDC's everyday surface mix (Cleaning and Disinfecting with Bleach), so choosing one fixed amount
   merges the two rows. Arithmetic (not a source): 1/3 cup gives about 960 ppm at 5% and about 1,700 ppm at 9%; 25
   tablespoons of 9% would be about 8,000 ppm, above CDC's 5,000 ceiling. Today: one strong mix, 1/3 cup of 5% to 9% per
   gallon; surfaces wet at least 1 minute, after vomit or diarrhea 10 minutes if you can, never under 5. Say if the
   group wants a stronger amount for vomit and diarrhea.

36. **Bleach mix for rodent droppings (surv-garbage-and-greywater) — Water pack final review.** CDC's rodent clean-up

37. **Generator distance from the house (surv-generators) — Heat pack Task 4.** CDC: more than 20 feet from windows,
   doors and vents. CPSC: at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust pointed away. FEMA and the NYS Division of Consumer
   Protection: at least 20 feet. NFPA's 2017 generator tip sheet: at least 5 feet from doors, windows and vent openings.
   Today: 20 feet (6 m) from windows, doors and vents, exhaust away from the house, as in Wells without power and the
   Carbon monoxide protocol; NFPA's 5 feet is not in the text. Say if the group wants a larger distance.
38. **Generators in rain and snow (surv-generators) — Heat pack Task 4.** OSHA (2005): "Keep a generator dry; do not use
   it in the rain or wet conditions." University of Illinois Extension: in wet weather use it only when necessary, under
   an open, canopy-like structure on a dry surface. Cornell: keep it dry; a canopy for a tractor-driven unit. NYS
   Division of Consumer Protection: where rain and snow can't reach it, if possible. CPSC: follow the maker's
   instructions. Today: OSHA's rule first, then the open canopy, with "never a shed, garage, porch or carport". A Central
   New York winter outage often means snow. Say whether the group wants a plain "don't run it in rain or snow" instead,
   or a sourced canopy design (none was found).
39. **Unvented heaters and fireplaces indoors (surv-portable-heaters, surv-carbon-monoxide) — Heat pack Task 4.** Agencies
   that advise against them: US Department of Energy (unvented combustion space heaters "not recommended for use inside
   your home"); NFPA (all fuel-burning equipment should be vented outside); NYS Department of Health (not your main heat
   source even in a power failure; a generator outdoors is better); New York City bans kerosene and propane space
   heaters (FDNY Foundation). New York law allows only approved kerosene heaters in a home, and none in a building with
   three or more families. CDC: "Don't cook or burn anything on a stove or fireplace that isn't vented," which read
   literally rules out a vent-free gas fireplace; NYS DOH only says it is a supplemental, not a primary, heat source.
   Ventilation: CPSC asks for the room's door open, and a window open about an inch when the door is closed or the house
   is tight or winterized; the app says always do both. Today: operating guidance under the strictest conditions found
   (the ruling in the heat pack ledger), with these agencies named first. The sleep rule is written one strict way in every place: turn it off when you go to sleep or leave the room, and never run it where anyone is sleeping (CPSC, USFA, Carbon monoxide protocol); say if the group wants to allow a heater tended by someone awake while others sleep elsewhere in the house. 40. **Electric heaters on a generator: extension cords (surv-generators) — Heat pack Task 4, revised in the fix round.**
   US Fire Administration (Heater Fire Safety card): "Plug portable heaters directly into outlets. Never use an extension
   cord or power strip." and "Do not plug anything else into the same circuit". CPSC: wall outlet, never a power strip.
   NYS DOH and NYS DHSES: avoid extension cords with electric heaters. US Department of Energy: if a cord is necessary,
   the shortest heavy-duty cord of 14-gauge wire or larger. With a generator outside and the heater inside, a cord is the
   usual way people would do it. Today: only through a transfer switch, heater in a wall outlet, nothing else on that
   circuit (USFA, the most cautious); DOE's cord is not in the text. Say whether the group wants DOE's 14-gauge cord
   allowed as a fallback when there is no transfer switch.
41. **Propane cylinders indoors (surv-portable-heaters, surv-store-fuel) — Heat pack Task 4, revised in the fix round.**
   North Carolina's LP-gas regulator (state law there) and the National Propane Gas Association: no propane cylinders
   indoors, nor in a garage or basement. CPSC: heaters with an oxygen depletion sensor, on 1 lb. cylinders, are designed
   for indoor use (it names tents, cabins and campers, not homes). Minnesota's State Fire Marshal, reading NFPA 58 (the
   national model code; found via Minnesota, not a New York source): in a home, garages included, no cylinder over 1 lb.
   and no more than 2 lb. in all, a connected cylinder counts as in use, and empties count as full. A 2003 NYS Department
   of State bulletin said LP-gas containers are prohibited inside buildings; its 2017 replacement does not say this, and
   the Fire Code of New York State's LP-gas chapter was not found in an opened source. Today: indoors only a 1 lb.
   cylinder screwed onto the heater, never a 20 lb. or larger tank and never one on a hose, empties back outside, spares
   outside; Minnesota's limit is quoted as another state's reading. Say if the group wants no cylinder indoors at all
   (which means no indoor propane heater).
42. **How much fuel to store, and for how long (surv-store-fuel) — Heat pack Task 4.** No New York household limit for
   gasoline, kerosene or propane was found in an opened source. The only storage time found is Maine DEP's: no more than
   30 days without a stabilizer (one made for ethanol gasoline). NYS DOH: don't burn last season's kerosene. Today: the
   topic says no New York limit was found and gives Maine's 30 days as the only figure. Say if someone in the group can
   ask the local fire marshal or code office for the limit, so it can be added from that source.
43. **Babies in a cold house (surv-heat-out, home-heat-kit) — Heat pack Task 5.** CDC: infants under 1 year "should
   never sleep in a cold room". University of Missouri Extension (GH5117): "Bed is the warmest place to be with other
   family members and lots of covers." Safe to Sleep (NICHD): only a fitted sheet in the baby's sleep area, no
   comforters, quilts, pillows or blankets; a wearable blanket or extra layer instead; too many layers overheat a baby
   (higher SIDS risk); couches and armchairs never; room sharing near but not in your bed is safer than bed-sharing.
   No source opened says how to keep a baby warm at night in a house with no heat. Today: the baby sleeps in the warm
   room, in its own sleep space, in a wearable blanket or extra layer, never in an adult bed under the family's covers
   (Missouri's line says "Not for babies"). Worth asking a medical professional what the group should do if the warm
   room itself gets cold overnight.
44. **Wood stove overnight (surv-wood-stoves-fireplaces) — Heat pack Task 5.** US Fire Administration (Fireplace and
   Wood Stove Safety card): "Put the fire out before you go to sleep or leave your home." Indiana Department of Homeland
   Security: extinguish fireplace fires before going to bed. CSIA: be sure the fire is fully extinguished before turning
   in. Penn State Extension describes banking a wood stove overnight on a bed of coals with the damper partly closed, and
   the US Department of Energy says oak, hickory and pine "will burn overnight". Today: put it out before sleep (USFA,
   the most cautious), and the text names Penn State's banking as described but not followed. With a stove as the only
   heat for weeks, this rule makes the nights cold. Say if the group wants a banked stove allowed overnight, and on what
   conditions (CO and smoke alarms, someone awake, damper never fully closed).
45. **When the house is too cold to stay (surv-heat-out) — Heat pack Task 5.** No source opened gives an indoor
   temperature at which to leave. CDC: "If you're not able to keep your home warm, make temporary arrangements to stay
   elsewhere"; infants under 1 never sleep in a cold room; over 65, check the temperature often. National Institute on
   Aging (2009): homes at 60 to 65°F can trigger hypothermia in older people. NYS Department of Health: keep indoor
   temperatures above 68°F for the very young, the elderly and people with health problems (and the heat no lower than
   55°F for pipes). Today: the topic gives CDC's rule and quotes those figures, and says no source gives a leave-at
   temperature. Say if the group wants its own line (for example, move babies and older people out when the warm room
   cannot be kept above a set temperature).
46. **Chimney fire: what to do (surv-wood-stoves-fireplaces) — Heat pack Task 5.** Crystal Lakes Fire Protection
   District (Colorado): call 911, get everyone out including pets, close stove doors and intake vents if you can safely,
   use a chimney-fire suppressant if you have one, have the fire department confirm it is out. University of Missouri
   Extension (G1731): call the fire department, close all openings and draft controls, "squirt a multi-purpose dry
   chemical extinguisher or throw baking soda onto the fire in the stove", never water into the chimney. No US Fire
   Administration, NFPA or New York page on chimney fires was found. Today: get everyone out and call if you can; close
   the doors and air intakes only if safe; never water into the chimney; have it checked; no reuse until inspected. The
   extinguisher and baking soda step is left out, in line with Store fuel safely's extinguisher rule (only a small fire in
   one object, with a clear way out). Say if the group wants Missouri's extinguisher step added.
60. **Fire beside a shelter: which way it faces and how far (surv-shelter, surv-fire) — Heat pack Task 7.** Army ATP
   3-50.21 (6-16, 6-32) puts the back of every lean-to into the wind, with the fire in front and a reflector behind it
   (5-7, 6-33). The same manual (6-40) says that when a fire is outside a shelter, the shelter's opening should face 90
   degrees to the prevailing wind, so sparks and smoke are not blown in when the wind reverses morning and evening (common
   in mountains), and that the best fire-to-shelter distance is about 3 feet. Smokey Bear: campfires at least 15 feet
   from tents (D8). Army TC 21-3: keep a lean-to fire from burning too hot and burning the shelter down. Today: the
   checked lean-to text stays (back into the wind, fire in front, reflector behind it); no distance is given; the new
   "Fire and stoves in and near shelters" section carries TC 21-3's warning. Say if the group wants the 90-degree
   opening or a distance added.
61. **Heating or cooking inside a snow shelter (surv-shelter) — Heat pack Task 7.** Army TC 21-3 (1986) heats snow caves
   and igloos with a one-burner gasoline stove or candles, puts them out while people sleep, and posts an awake fire guard
   if a fire is needed while sleeping. Army ATP 3-50.21 says to cook in the lower entrance area of a snow cave (6-27) and
   that candles, fuel gel and small oil lamps make carbon monoxide, so vent holes must stay open (6-39). The US Antarctic
   Program's 2024 field manual says ventilation is critical if a stove is run in any snow shelter. CDC: never use a
   charcoal grill, lantern or portable camping stove inside a tent; CPSC: never use fuel-burning heaters or lanterns while
   sleeping in an enclosed area. Today: no stove, grill or lantern inside a tent or snow shelter at all, and no heater or
   lantern while sleeping in one (the checked Shelter and Priorities line, CDC and CPSC); the topic says the Army
   manuals differ and that the app does not follow them. Melting snow for water stays outdoors (Water in winter).
62. **Pyramid fire overnight (surv-fire) — Heat pack Task 7.** Army ATP 3-50.21 (5-27): a pyramid fire burns downward,
   "requiring no attention during the night." USDA Forest Service, Smokey Bear and NYS DEC: never leave a fire
   unattended (the checked Fire text). Today: the pyramid lay is described, the Army's claim is named, and the text says
